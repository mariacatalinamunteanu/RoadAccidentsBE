PRESENTATION – Road accidents in Belgium 2017–2024
"Is it true that as the density of the population increases, the severity of the accidents decreases? Why?"

-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. QUESTION & CONCEPT  (1:00)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SAY:
- "My question: in Belgium, do accidents become less severe as population density increases? And if
  so, why?"
- "Two definitions are key:
    * A severe accident is accident class 1, 2 or 3: with deaths, fatally injured or seriously injured.
      Class 4 means only light injuries.
    * Severity Ratio = severe accidents / all recorded injury accidents x 100. Each accident counts once."
- "I needed to bring three things together: WHERE accidents happen and how severe they are, HOW DENSELY
  POPULATED that place is, and WHERE the centre of each area is, so I can put it on a map."
- "The work has three layers: raw data -> a Python script that cleans and combines it -> a Streamlit app
  that shows the data quality and answers the question."

  Raw data (3 sources) --> pipeline.py (one pure function per step) --> Processed data --> app.py (Streamlit)


-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. DATA SOURCES  (1:30)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SAY: "All three sources come from Statbel, the Belgian statistical office."

a) Accidents – "Geolocatie van de verkeersongevallen 2017–2024"
   - 289,532 injury accidents, 45 columns. Every column exists in French and Dutch.
   - Per accident: year, month, hour, municipality code (CD_NIS = NIS/REFNIS code), region, province,
     municipality, X/Y coordinates, weather, road condition, built-up area or not, light, road type,
     accident class, road users, type of collision, obstacle.
   - The coordinates are in Belgian Lambert 72 (EPSG:31370): metres, not GPS degrees.
   - It uses 581 municipalities: the codes after the 2019 mergers, for all years.

b) Population density
   - Pop_density_en.xlsx: density per municipality, but only for 2019–2026.
   - Bevolking_per_gemeente.xlsx: population on 1 January per municipality, 1992–2026.
   - Problem: 2017 and 2018 have no density file AND they use the old municipality codes (589).
     Solution: I map the old codes to the 2019 codes, add up the population and divide by the 2019 area.
       * 7 mergers, e.g. Neerpelt + Overpelt -> Pelt, Puurs + Sint-Amands -> Puurs-Sint-Amands,
         Lovendegem + Waarschoot + Zomergem -> Lievegem.
       * Hainaut municipalities that got a new code in 2019 (same name) -> matched by name.
   - Check: the Belgian total for 2017 = 11,322,088, which matches Statbel.

c) Geography – statistical sectors 2024 (19,795 small areas with their borders, Lambert 72)
   - I merge (in GIS terms: "dissolve") the sectors into the 581 municipalities.
   - Centre of a municipality = centroid of its polygon. For 5 oddly shaped municipalities the centroid
     falls outside the municipality, so there I use a point guaranteed to be inside ("representative point").


-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. DATA PROCESSING – pipeline.py  (2:00)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[SHOW] The row funnel at the top of the app, then tab "Pipeline & method" -> "Steps (rows in/out)".
SAY: "pipeline.py does all the processing, one pure function per step. Each step reports rows in, rows out,
     rows dropped or changed, and why. Steps 1-7 drop 0 rows; step 8 leaves 36,628 accidents (12.7%) out of the
     GPS heat layer only. Every number in the app has a 'Source' line saying which step created it."

Step 1 – Language: remove the 14 French columns; we work only in Dutch.

Step 2 – Province for Brussels: TX_PROV_COLLISION_NL is empty for 30,022 rows. These are all in Brussels,
         because Brussels does not belong to a province. I fill them with "Brussels Hoofdstedelijk Gewest",
         so Brussels appears as its own unit in the province view.

Step 3 – Population density: join on municipality code + accident year -> new columns POPULATION,
         AREA_KM2, POP_DENSITY (inhabitants/km², on 1 January of that year). 0 rows without a match.

Step 4 – Coordinate check. Three kinds of abnormal values:
           * missing ............................................ 35,881
           * impossible (around 10^16, far outside Belgium) ......... 2
           * more than 1 km outside the municipality in CD_NIS ...... 745
         -> 36,628 rows (12.7%) get the coordinates of the centre of their own municipality.
         How do I know a point is "outside"? I calculate the distance from each point to the border of
         the municipality that is recorded for that accident.
         Why a 1 km tolerance? 3,932 points are just slightly outside, usually on a border road.
         That is normal geocoding, so I keep them.
         For transparency I keep the original coordinates (MS_X/Y_COORD_ORIG) plus COORD_STATUS,
         COORD_IMPUTED and DIST_TO_OWN_MUNTY_M, and I add LAT/LON (WGS84) for the maps.

Output: accidents_processed_2017-2024.csv (289,532 rows x 41 columns),
        population_density_2017-2024.csv (581 municipalities x 8 years = 4,648 rows),
        municipality_centers_2024.csv, municipalities_2024.geojson, processing_log.txt.

5. APP – ANSWER TO THE QUESTION  (2:30)  [live demo]
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[SHOW] Tab "Density vs severity" – the green box at the top.
SAY: "Short answer: YES. In municipalities with fewer than 250 inhabitants/km², 13.6% of injury accidents
     are severe. Above 5,000 inhabitants/km² it is only 4.9%. Across all 581 municipalities the Spearman
     correlation is -0.50, which is clearly negative and significant."

[SHOW] The heat map. Click through: Regions -> Provinces -> Municipalities -> BE.
SAY: "How to read it:
  - Each dot sits at the centre of a region, province or municipality, and it is recalculated for every
    button.
  - Colour = Severity Ratio, from blue (low) to red (high). I avoided red–green so it is also readable for
    colour-blind viewers.
  - Size = population density.
  - The grey heat layer shows where the accidents actually happened, based on their GPS coordinates.
  On the province view you can see it immediately: Brussels is a big blue dot (very dense, low ratio).
  Luxembourg and West Flanders are small red dots (less dense, high ratio)."
  Behind the scenes: for each unit I count accidents and severe accidents, and calculate the density as
  total population / total area of the municipalities in it (averaged over the selected years). The centre
  of a region or province = centroid of the merged municipality borders.

[SHOW] Scatter plot next to the map (Municipalities view).
SAY: "Each dot is a municipality. There is a minimum-accidents filter, because a municipality with 5
     accidents has an unreliable ratio. The dashed line is a trend weighted by the number of accidents."

[SHOW] Scroll to "Why?" – 4 charts.
SAY: "Why does severity go down?
  1. Speed, the main reason. In the least dense municipalities, 61% of accidents happen outside built-up
     areas, on 70–90 km/h roads. In the densest it is 2%. Crash energy rises with the square of the speed.
  2. Type of crash. Less dense areas have more single-vehicle crashes and crashes into trees and posts
     (37% vs 12%). These are among the most severe. In the city it is mostly rear-end and side collisions
     at low speed.
  3. Even INSIDE built-up areas the ratio falls with density (10.5% -> 4.9%): congestion, 30 km/h zones,
     traffic lights, street lighting, and hospitals and ambulances close by.
  4. Recording: cities have many more small accidents and police nearby to record them. The denominator
     grows, so the ratio goes down. This is a hypothesis; the data cannot prove it."


-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. CONCLUSION  (1:00)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SAY:
- "Yes: the denser the area, the lower the share of severe accidents. The ratio goes down from about 14%
  to 5%."
- "Not because city people drive better, but because WHERE and HOW accidents happen changes: lower
  speeds, fewer single-vehicle crashes, more lighting, and help arriving faster."
- "Watch out: there are MORE accidents in dense areas. They are just less often severe."
- "Limitations: this compares areas, not individual drivers (the ecological fallacy). Small municipalities
  give noisy ratios. And 12.7% of coordinates were replaced, but that does not affect the ratios, because
  I group by municipality code, not by GPS position."
- "Everything can be reproduced: one script for the processing, one app for the analysis."


=====================================================================================================
BACKUP – LIKELY QUESTIONS
=====================================================================================================
Q: Why Lambert 72 and not GPS directly?
A: Statbel delivers the data in Lambert 72 (metres), which makes distance calculations simple. For the
   maps I convert to WGS84 (latitude/longitude) with pyproj/geopandas.

Q: Why replace the coordinates instead of deleting the rows?
A: The accident itself is valid; we know the municipality. Deleting would lose 12.7% of the accidents and
   bias the ratios. The centre of the municipality is the best available estimate. The COORD_IMPUTED flag
   keeps it transparent.

Q: Why 1 km as the threshold?
A: It is a judgement call. Up to 1 km is typically a point on a border road. It is one parameter in the
   script (OUTSIDE_TOLERANCE_M), so it is easy to change.

Q: Why did you use Spearman and not Pearson?
A: Density is very skewed (24 to 23,000 inh./km²) and the relation is not linear. Spearman uses ranks,
   so it is robust to that. (Pearson on log10 density gives a similar result, about -0.54.)

Q: Is the result significant?
A: Yes, p < 0.001 across 581 municipalities.

Q: Do the 66 duplicate rows matter?
A: No. 66 out of 289,532 is 0.02%. They are probably genuine separate accidents, since the data only has
   the hour, not the minute.

Q: Why use the 2019 area for 2017–2018?
A: The areas of municipalities barely change over time, and the accident data already uses the 2019
   municipalities. The check shows 2018 vs 2019 density differs by -0.4% on average, which is just
   population growth.

Q: What does "BE" show on the map?
A: One dot for all of Belgium (density about 380 inh./km²). You cannot see a relationship with one point,
   so the app says so and refers to the other views.

Q: What would you do next?
A: A statistical model (e.g. logistic regression: severe yes/no) with density, built-up area, road type,
   light, and road users at the same time. That would show whether density still has an effect once
   speed and road type are taken into account.

Q: How do you know the numbers are right?
A: Two kinds of tests (tests/). 15 unit tests on the pipeline functions with small hand-made data, and 8 tests that
   fill what those miss (tests/TEST_GAPS.md): an independent recalculation of the Severity Ratio from the raw file,
   replaced coordinates lie inside their own municipality, all border views count the same accidents, population
   equals Statbel's published totals, the funnel matches the data, and the app runs every button.

Q: Where are your choices written down?
A: CLAUDE.md (the project agreements), decision_log.md (each decision: what, why, alternatives, impact) and in the app:
   tab "Pipeline & method" -> Assumptions, Category rules and Decision log.

Q: Which tools did you use?
A: Python: pandas (data), geopandas/shapely/pyproj (geo), scipy (statistics), Plotly (charts and maps,
   on a Carto map background), Streamlit (app).
